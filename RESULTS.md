# RESULTS.md: washing-machine EDA, model results and failure analysis

The data is the official cleaned REFIT data at 1-minute resolution. It covers **12 houses** (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 and 21) over each house's full recorded period, Sep 2013 – Jul 2015 (per-house dates are in DATA.md §4). The houses yield **4,566 detected washing-machine cycles**. Every number below is regenerated by `python run_all.py`.

## Part 2: Exploratory analysis of washing-machine use

### Cycle rule (plain language)

> A minute counts as **running** when the washing machine's average power is **above 20 W**, which is well above standby (0–5 W).
> A wash has quiet phases (soaking, draining, pauses between tumbles), so running minutes separated by a pause of **15 minutes or less** are joined into one cycle.
> A joined block counts as a **cycle** only if it lasts **at least 10 minutes**, uses **at least 0.05 kWh** and reaches **at least 200 W** at some point. This removes blips such as the door lock or the display waking up.

The 20 W on-threshold matches what NILM work on washing machines commonly uses (e.g. Kelly & Knottenbelt, 2015). The rule is in `src/cycles.py`, and every threshold is in `src/config.py`.

### Availability and data quality (summary; full table in DATA.md §4)

* Each house has 15–21 months of data. Between 6% and 25% of minutes are missing (House 2 is the worst, with 78 gaps over 1 h and one 62-day outage).
* The washing machine is off (≤ 20 W) for **92–99%** of available minutes and draws exactly 0 W for 52–99%. House 5's machine idles at a few watts rather than 0.
* Washes per week range from **2.2 (House 18)** to **10.9 (House 7)**, and washing-machine energy from 0.12 to 0.91 kWh/day, which is 1–7% of household consumption.

## Part 3: Disaggregation model

### Task, homes and periods

Estimate the washing machine's power each minute from the **household aggregate only**, at 1-minute resolution. All 12 houses above are used over their full periods: 8.4 M usable minutes in total, after dropping minutes with a missing window or a physically impossible target (DATA.md §4).

### Model: a seq2point-style gradient-boosted regressor (`src/part3_model.py`)

* **Idea from the literature:** sequence-to-point learning (Zhang et al., 2018). Predict the appliance's power at the *midpoint* of a window of aggregate readings, so the model can see both how a cycle starts and how it ends. I kept the windowing idea but replaced the CNN with **XGBoost** on hand-made window features. That is faster to train, simpler to explain, and model complexity is not assessed.
* **Features** (all from the aggregate in a ±30 min window): 31 lags; level above the local 2-hour minimum, which removes each house's different always-on load; rolling mean, std, min and max over 5–61 min; the fraction of time above 1.5 kW; first difference and its rolling absolute sum; plus hour of day and weekend. The time features are tested by an ablation below.
* **Training sample:** every minute within 60 min of a real cycle, plus a random 10% of all other minutes. Those other minutes get a **weight of 10**, so the loss still reflects the true balance between on and off minutes.
* **Post-processing:** predictions are clipped to `[0, aggregate]`, values below 20 W become 0, and a **cycle filter** keeps predicted power only inside predicted blocks that pass the same cycle rule as Part 2. This removes short false alarms.

### Validation design: leave-one-house-out (12 folds)

Each house is the **test home exactly once**. In that fold it contributes nothing to training, early stopping or threshold choice. Within the 11 training houses, the **last 20% of each house's timeline** is held out for early stopping and for choosing the zeroing threshold.

*How this avoids leakage:* neighbouring 1-minute windows overlap by 60 of 61 minutes. A random split would put almost identical windows in both train and test and grossly overstate accuracy. Splitting by **household** guarantees the test home's appliances, habits and wiring are never seen, which is also the real deployment setting (a new customer with no sub-metering). The inner split is by **time**, so early stopping cannot peek at future data from the same home either.

### Baselines

* **Always-off:** predict 0 W. This is hard to beat on plain per-minute error, because the machine is off more than 90% of the time.
* **Hour-of-week profile:** for each weekday and hour, the average washing-machine power across the training houses. It knows typical habits but not the test home's aggregate.

### Metrics and why

| Metric | What it tells a utility |
|---|---|
| **Daily energy MAE (kWh/day)** | How wrong the estimated washing-machine energy per day is. This is the closest to what a bill insight needs. |
| **Total energy error (%)** | Bias over the whole period, i.e. whether the model systematically over- or under-estimates. |
| **Cycle recall / precision** | Whether we catch each wash, and how many "washes" we report are real. This is what event-based insights (counting washes, timing) need. |
| Minute F1 (on = > 20 W) | Whether the on/off state is right minute by minute |
| MAE (W), and MAE during on-minutes | Standard NILM point error. It is dominated by the off-time and favours always-off, so it is reported but not used alone. |

### Interpretation

* **The model clearly beats both baselines on every metric a utility would use.** Daily energy error is 28% lower than always-off and better in 10 of 12 homes. It finds **81% of real washes** in homes it has never seen, and minute F1 is four times that of the usage profile.
* **Per-minute MAE is the one metric where always-off wins** (16 W vs 21 W). With the machine off 92–99% of the time, any false alarm costs more than predicting zero everywhere. This is a known weakness of MAE for rarely used appliances, and the reason MAE is not the headline metric.
* **The main weakness is false alarms.** Only about 39% of predicted washes are real, because other heating loads look like the 2 kW plateau. Estimates for a *single* home can be off by ±50–100% in total energy. Results should be reported as aggregated or ranked insights, not exact per-home kWh.
* **Ablation:** removing the hour and weekend features barely changes anything (F1 0.43 → 0.42). The model recognises the *shape* of a wash in the aggregate rather than memorising when people wash, which is what lets it generalise to new homes. The most important features (`outputs/model_feature_importance.csv`) are level above the local baseline, the 5-minute minimum (a sustained load, not a spike) and the time spent above 1.5 kW in 15–61 minute windows.
* The cycle filter mainly removes false energy: mean signed total error drops from +16% to +0.6%, and minute F1 rises from 0.39 to 0.43.

### Failure cases

1. **A washing machine with an unusual signature, House 19** (`figures/p3_actual_vs_pred_h19.png`). This machine heats in short 1.3–1.6 kW bursts instead of one long 2 kW plateau, and mostly at night. None of the 11 training homes has that pattern, so the model misses 83% of its washes. On the same day it fires on two 2.4 kW plateaus from another appliance (13:40 and 18:45), likely a dishwasher or oven. *Lesson:* an unusual machine in a new home is the biggest risk. A small amount of labelled data or user feedback from that home ("did you wash today?") would help most.

   ![h19](figures/p3_actual_vs_pred_h19.png)

2. **Busy homes with overlapping heating loads, House 3 (+62%) and House 21 (+62%)** (`figures/p3_actual_vs_pred_h3.png`). When the aggregate is full of 2–6 kW loads (electric shower, cooker, tumble dryer or dishwasher heating), several of them get labelled as washing. Washes that overlap another big load are also under-estimated, because the plateau is hidden inside a larger block. House 1 is a related case: its washer-dryer (not part of the target) produces true washing-like cycles that the model correctly sees but that count as false positives.

   ![h3](figures/p3_actual_vs_pred_h3.png)

### Research used

| Paper | What I took from it |
|---|---|
| Murray, Stankovic & Stankovic (2017), *An electrical load measurements dataset of United Kingdom households from a two-year longitudinal study*, Scientific Data 4:160122 | Dataset description, IAM limitations, the Issues flag (which I then tested and decided not to use) |
| Zhang, Zhong, Wang, Goddard & Sutton (2018), *Sequence-to-point learning with neural networks for non-intrusive load monitoring*, AAAI | Predicting the midpoint of an aggregate window; evaluating on unseen REFIT houses |
| Kelly & Knottenbelt (2015), *Neural NILM: Deep neural networks applied to energy disaggregation*, ACM BuildSys | Per-appliance on-power threshold (~20 W for washing machines); training on windows that are rebalanced toward activations; reporting both energy and on/off metrics |
| Makonin & Popowich (2015), *Nonintrusive load monitoring (NILM) performance evaluation*, Energy Efficiency 8 | Why accuracy and MAE mislead for sparse appliances, and why energy-based and state-based metrics should be reported side by side |
| D'Incecco, Squartini & Zhong (2019), *Transfer learning for non-intrusive load monitoring*, IEEE Trans. Smart Grid | Cross-house generalisation is the realistic test, and the motivation for the leave-one-house-out design |

### What I would do next

1. **Event-level confirmation:** a second-stage classifier on each predicted cycle as a whole (duration, energy, tail shape, time since last wash) to cut the 61% false-alarm rate. It would use the same LOHO validation.
2. **A Seq2Point CNN** with the same folds, to see whether learned features capture the low tail better than hand-made ones.
3. **Add the remaining REFIT houses** (6, 8, 9, 10, 13, 20) after manually checking their channel labels, so more machine types are seen in training.
4. **Probabilistic outputs**, such as an interval on monthly washing-machine kWh, so a utility can decide when an insight is confident enough to show a customer.
